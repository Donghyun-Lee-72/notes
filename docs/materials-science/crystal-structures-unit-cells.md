# Crystal Structures and Unit Cells

In the periodic structures considered here, a **crystal structure** specifies
a representative group of particles and the translations that reproduce it
throughout three-dimensional space. This repeating description reduces an
enormous solid to a manageable geometric model. It also helps connect atomic
geometry and direction to density, deformation, diffusion, electrical
response, and other properties. The model does not imply that every repeated
site in a real specimen is flawless
(Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §§3.2–3.5, pp. 49–57;
[IUCr, “Crystal”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Crystal)).

## Separate the lattice from what occupies it

Four related terms answer different questions:

- A **lattice** is an infinite mathematical array of equivalent points. It
  describes translational periodicity and has no chemical identity by itself.
- A **basis**, also called a **motif**, is the atom, ion, molecule, or group of
  particles attached in the same orientation to every lattice point.
- The **crystal structure** is generated by placing the basis at every lattice
  point: `crystal structure = lattice + basis`.
- A **unit cell** is a parallelepiped defined by three non-coplanar translation
  vectors. Translating it by integer multiples of those vectors tiles space
  without gaps or overlaps.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: a point at the center of a drawn
cell does not automatically make the structure a body-centred Bravais lattice.
The chemical basis must also be considered. The IUCr explicitly treats a
lattice as a mathematical description of periodicity rather than an atomic
substructure, and defines a unit cell through three crystallographic basis
vectors
([IUCr, “Substructure”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Substructure);
[IUCr, “Unit cell”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Unit_cell)).

The cell vectors have lengths `a`, `b`, and `c`. The interaxial angles are
`α = ∠(b,c)`, `β = ∠(a,c)`, and `γ = ∠(a,b)`. These six **lattice
parameters** specify cell geometry; particle identities and fractional
coordinates specify the basis.

## Primitive and conventional cells serve different purposes

A **primitive cell** contains exactly one lattice point after boundary sharing
is counted. It has the smallest volume that can reproduce the lattice by
translation. It need not display the lattice symmetry in the most recognizable
way.

A **conventional cell** is chosen to make symmetry and geometry easy to see.
It may be non-primitive and contain more than one lattice point. For example,
the familiar cubic conventional cells for body-centred and face-centred
lattices contain two and four lattice points, respectively. Both choices
describe the same infinite lattice when their vectors and contents are stated
consistently. “Unit cell” therefore does not always mean “smallest cell”
([IUCr, “Primitive cell”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Primitive_cell);
[IUCr, “Centred lattice”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Centred_lattice)).

## Count particles by ownership, not by appearance

A cell boundary is a bookkeeping surface, not a physical cut through an atom.
For a cubic conventional cell, a site wholly inside contributes `1`, a
face-centred site contributes `1/2`, an edge-centred site contributes `1/4`,
and a corner site contributes `1/8`. Thus

`N = N_i + N_f/2 + N_e/4 + N_c/8`,

where `N` is the effective number of particles in the cell and `N_i`, `N_f`,
`N_e`, and `N_c` are the numbers drawn at interior, face, edge, and corner
positions. The sharing fraction depends on the cell geometry: a corner of the
conventional hexagonal prism is shared among six such prisms, not eight.

For a multi-species structure, count each species separately. A correct total
atom count can still hide an incorrect chemical formula.

## Compare SC, BCC, FCC, and HCP as geometric models

The following results assume identical, rigid spheres that touch along the
stated directions. `R` is the sphere radius and `a` is the cubic cell edge.
**Coordination number** is the number of nearest neighbours at the same
nearest-neighbour distance. The **atomic packing factor** (APF) is

`APF = (volume of the hard spheres assigned to the cell)/(cell volume)`.

It is dimensionless. APF is not a measured porosity and does not count electron
density; it is a geometric consequence of the equal-sphere model (Callister and
Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §3.4, pp. 51–56;
[LibreTexts, §12.2](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/General_Chemistry/Book%253A_General_Chemistry%253A_Principles_Patterns_and_Applications_%28Averill%29/12%253A_Solids/12.02%253A_The_Arrangement_of_Atoms_in_Crystalline_Solids)).

| Structure and conventional cell | Effective particles `N` | Contact geometry | Coordination | APF |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Simple cubic (SC) | `8(1/8) = 1` | edge: `a = 2R` | 6 | `π/6 ≈ 0.52` |
| Body-centred cubic (BCC) | `8(1/8) + 1 = 2` | body diagonal: `√3a = 4R` | 8 | `√3π/8 ≈ 0.68` |
| Face-centred cubic (FCC) | `8(1/8) + 6(1/2) = 4` | face diagonal: `√2a = 4R` | 12 | `π/(3√2) ≈ 0.74` |

The algebra provides a useful audit. For FCC, for example,
`a = 2√2R`, so the cell volume is `a^3 = 16√2 R^3`.
Four spheres occupy `4(4πR^3/3) = 16πR^3/3`; their ratio is
`π/(3√2) ≈ 0.7405`.

### Hexagonal close-packed structure

The **hexagonal close-packed (HCP)** structure stacks close-packed planes in an
`ABAB…` sequence. FCC can be viewed as the alternative `ABCABC…` close-packed
sequence. Both ideal equal-sphere packings have coordination number 12 and APF
`π/(3√2) ≈ 0.74`, but their translational symmetries and stacking repeat differ
([LibreTexts, §12.2](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/General_Chemistry/Book%253A_General_Chemistry%253A_Principles_Patterns_and_Applications_%28Averill%29/12%253A_Solids/12.02%253A_The_Arrangement_of_Atoms_in_Crystalline_Solids);
Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §§3.4 and 3.16, pp. 53–56, 89–90).

The conventional hexagonal-prism depiction assigns
`12(1/6) + 2(1/2) + 3 = 6` atoms. For ideal touching spheres,
`a = 2R` and

`c/a = √(8/3) ≈ 1.633`,

where `c` is the prism height. Real HCP crystals can have a different `c/a`
ratio, so “HCP” does not guarantee ideal sphere geometry. The six-atom prism
is a convenient non-primitive cell; primitive and conventional descriptions
must not be mixed in one count
(Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §3.4, pp. 54–55).

## Compute crystallographic density with a declared cell

For a one-component crystal,

`ρ = nM/(N_A V_cell)`,

where:

- `ρ` is crystallographic density;
- `n` is the number of atoms assigned to the chosen cell;
- `M` is molar mass in `g mol^-1`;
- `N_A = 6.022 140 76 × 10^23 mol^-1` is the exact Avogadro constant; and
- `V_cell` is cell volume in `cm^3` when `ρ` is required in `g cm^-3`.

For a compound, replace `nM` by `Z M_formula`, where `Z` is the number of
formula units per cell and `M_formula` is molar mass per formula unit. The
numerator and volume must refer to the same cell. The relationship follows from
mass per cell divided by volume per cell
(Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §§3.5 and 3.7, pp. 57, 63–64;
[BIPM, “Mole”](https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole)).

### Independent numerical audit

Consider a hypothetical monatomic FCC solid with `M = 60.0 g mol^-1` and
`a = 0.400 nm`. These are teaching inputs, not data for a real material.
Because `n = 4` and `1 nm = 10^-7 cm`,

`V_cell = (0.400 × 10^-7 cm)^3 = 6.40 × 10^-23 cm^3`,

and

`ρ = [4(60.0 g mol^-1)]/[(6.022 140 76 × 10^23 mol^-1)(6.40 × 10^-23 cm^3)]`

`= 6.23 g cm^-3`.

The mole units and the powers of ten cancel correctly. As a reverse check,
`ρV_cell = 3.99 × 10^-22 g`, equal after rounding to
`4M/N_A`, the mass of four model atoms. This calculation is a perfect-cell
prediction. Vacancies, substitutions, nonstoichiometry, thermal expansion,
multiple phases, and porosity can make measured bulk density differ.

## Place cells within crystal systems and Bravais lattices

The seven introductory **crystal systems** are triclinic, monoclinic,
orthorhombic, tetragonal, trigonal, hexagonal, and cubic. They classify
three-dimensional crystal symmetry; their conventional cells are described
using constraints on `a`, `b`, `c`, `α`, `β`, and `γ`. Combining the allowed
symmetries with primitive and permitted centring choices gives the 14
three-dimensional **Bravais lattice types**. A Bravais lattice classifies
translation symmetry, not the chemical basis and not the complete crystal
structure
([IUCr educational pamphlet 2](https://www.iucr.org/education/pamphlets/2/full-text)).

At this depth, the important point is hierarchy:

`crystallographic symmetry + conventional-cell metric constraints → crystal system; translation and centring → Bravais lattice; lattice + basis → crystal structure`.

SC, BCC, and FCC are the three cubic Bravais lattice types. HCP is a crystal
structure built on a primitive hexagonal Bravais lattice with a basis; it is
not a separate Bravais lattice. Detailed point groups, space groups, and setting
conventions belong to a later crystallography treatment.

## Structure makes direction matter

A property is **anisotropic** when its measured value depends on direction in
the specimen; it is **isotropic** when it does not. Even a cubic crystal is not
automatically isotropic for every property. Symmetry constrains which
directional values must be equal, while the property’s tensor character
determines the remaining possibilities.

A single crystal can therefore show direction-dependent elastic, optical,
electrical, magnetic, or transport response. A polycrystal with many randomly
oriented grains may appear approximately isotropic at a scale containing many
grains, but preferred orientation (**texture**), grain shape, phases, or
processing can preserve macroscopic anisotropy. Random orientation is an
assumption to test, not a consequence of being polycrystalline
(Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §3.19, pp. 92–94).

## One composition can have more than one structure

**Polymorphism** means that a substance of one chemical composition can occur
in more than one crystal structure. When this structural multiplicity occurs
in an elemental substance rather than a compound, it is called **allotropy**.
Which form is stable can depend on temperature and pressure, and transformations
may be kinetically delayed. A structural change can alter density and other
properties without changing the overall composition
(Callister and Rethwisch, 2015, Ch. 3, §3.10, pp. 68–69;
[IUPAC, “Allotropes”](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00243)).

## Know what the ideal model leaves out

- Perfect periodicity omits surfaces, vacancies, interstitials, substitutions,
  dislocations, grain boundaries, disorder, and thermal motion.
- Hard spheres assign a sharp radius and pairwise contact. Real electron
  density is continuous, bonding can be directional, and an effective radius
  depends on chemical environment.
- Coordination requires a stated neighbour rule. In distorted or multi-species
  structures, a distance cutoff can be ambiguous.
- APF compares equal hard-sphere volumes inside an ideal cell. It is neither
  bulk porosity nor a universal measure of stability.
- A cell drawing is not unique. Primitive and conventional descriptions can
  both be correct, but counts, coordinates, and volumes cannot be mixed.
- Crystallographic density is a model value. Agreement with a bulk measurement
  requires representative composition, phase content, temperature, and
  negligible unaccounted pore volume.
- Three-dimensional translational periodicity covers the conventional crystals
  used in this note, but the modern crystallographic definition also includes
  long-range-ordered aperiodic crystals
  ([IUCr, “Crystal”](https://dictionary.iucr.org/Crystal)).
